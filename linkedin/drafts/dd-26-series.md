## Post 1 · AI is already in the room

```
72% of physicians now use AI in their work.
The most common use isn't diagnosis. It's reading.

That's from the AMA's 2026 survey of US physicians. In 2023 the same figure was 38%.

Look at what they actually use it for:

Summarizing research and standards of care: 39%
Discharge instructions and care plans: 30%
Documentation: 28%
Chart summaries: 28%
Assistive diagnosis: 12%

Hospitals moved the same way. 71% of US acute-care hospitals ran predictive AI inside their EHR in 2024. The fastest-growing use wasn't clinical. It was billing, up from 36% to 61% of hospitals using AI.

So AI didn't arrive in medicine through the scanner. It arrived through the reading list and the back office, one workflow at a time.

That matters for anyone responsible for clinical teams. A tool that arrives with a launch gets a policy and a training plan. A tool that arrives quietly is already in use on Monday morning, by someone deciding how far to trust what it says.

The question isn't whether your clinicians use AI anymore. It's whether anyone showed them how to use it well.

Sources in the first comment.
```

## Post 2 · What the trials actually measured

```
AI in clinical work now has randomized trial data behind it.
The measured gains are real, and smaller than the marketing.

Breast screening. The MASAI trial randomized 105,934 women in Sweden. AI-supported screening found 6.4 cancers per 1,000 women versus 5.0 with standard double reading, with no significant rise in false positives. Radiologists' screen-reading workload fell 44%. The caveat: on the trial's primary endpoint, interval cancers, AI was non-inferior. Not worse, but not shown to be better.

Documentation. A randomized trial of 238 physicians across 14 specialties tested two ambient AI scribes. One cut time spent in notes by 9.5%. The other didn't make a significant difference. Work exhaustion improved modestly for physicians using either one. The authors also saw occasional clinically significant inaccuracies. They concluded the tools need ongoing physician vigilance.

Attention. In a Singapore study of 169 consultations, a scribe cut documentation time per visit by 15% and raised eye contact with the patient from 69.6% to 77.1% of the visit. Total time per patient didn't change.

So the evidence today points to less workload, more attention on the patient, and better detection in specific, well-studied settings. Not a different kind of medicine.

That's worth having. But only if the clinician using the tool knows where it was tested, and where it wasn't.

Next time someone shows you an AI result, ask three things. What was the effect size? In which population? What was the caveat?
```

## Post 3 · They have it. They weren't taught it.

```
72% of physicians use AI in their work.
27% have had no AI training from any source.

Both numbers come from the same AMA survey, and of the physicians who have had some training, only 11% would call it "a lot".

Training barely tracks age: 74% of physicians under 35 have had some, against 71% of those over 55. It tracks the employer: 79% in hospitals, 55% in solo practice.

Globally the gap is wider. In a 2024 survey of 1,049 physicians across 50 countries, 86.5% said they understood AI at least at a basic level. 17.7% had formal training. Those who had it were 3.4 times more likely to use AI in practice. That's an association. It doesn't prove training causes use.

Here's why the gap matters.

In a randomized trial, 50 physicians worked through diagnostic cases with or without an LLM. With it, they scored 76%. Without it, 74%. Not a significant difference. The LLM on its own scored 16 points higher than the physicians did. A capable tool, a capable doctor, and the pairing added almost nothing. (Written cases, not patients, and a model that's now two generations old.)

Skills can also move the wrong way. At four endoscopy centers in Poland, adenoma detection in standard colonoscopy, with no AI switched on, fell from 28.4% to 22.4% after AI was introduced. It's observational, and the authors say exposure "might" reduce skill. But 88% of physicians in the AMA survey already say they worry about skill loss.

Having the tool isn't the skill. Knowing what it's good at, where it fails, and when to overrule it is.

92% of physicians say they want more training in exactly that.
```

## Post 4 · What the market offers a clinician in 2026

> **Check before posting:** the Harvard, Coursera, OpenEvidence, UpToDate,
> Relias and HealthStream details were read on 17 Sep 2026 and not
> re-verified. Re-open each page before this goes out (evidence.md §D, §F).

```
If you're a clinician who wants to learn to use AI well, here's what's actually on offer in 2026. Each option is genuinely good at something.

Society CME. The AMA's "AI in Health Care" series: six modules, up to 3.5 AMA PRA Category 1 credits. Credible and accredited.

University programs. Harvard Medical School's AI in Clinical Medicine: three live days, $2,500, up to 27 credits. Depth and a strong credential. Cost and time are the constraint.

Online platforms. Stanford's AI in Healthcare on Coursera: five courses, about 48 hours, free to audit. Accessible, but not specialty-specific, and no CME credit.

Compliance training. Packaged EU AI Act courses, such as NAVEX's, sold to organizations. Useful for a regulator. Written for employees in general, not for clinicians.

Credit for using a tool. OpenEvidence and UpToDate Expert AI now award CME credit for using their AI. Free and frictionless. The credit rewards use and reflection, not whether you'd catch a wrong answer.

Hospital learning systems. Relias and HealthStream hold the completion record in many hospitals. Excellent at assignment and audit. When we checked their public pages in September 2026, we didn't find a clinician-facing AI curriculum.

Now compare that with what physicians say they want. In the AMA's 2026 survey, they picked training inside the EHR (49%) and on the job (46%) first, then online CE courses (40%) and a hands-on sandbox to try the tools (39%). Training from AI companies themselves came last, at 14%.

So there's no shortage of AI courses. What's rare is what physicians describe: close to their real work, hands-on, short enough to finish. And, we'd add, built around their specialty rather than AI in general.
```

```
Everything in this series points to one conclusion: the tool isn't the skill.

So that's what we built Darman.ai around.

Specialty-specific courses, created with physicians who show how they actually use generative AI in their own field. What goes into the prompt, what comes out, what they double-check, and when they decide not to use AI at all.

Limitations taught as seriously as use cases. Verification, failure modes, and the situations where clinical judgment has to take over.

Short lessons, built to be finished between patients, with a final assessment you need 70% to pass. Not a video you let play.

Darman Labs: eight AI tools where clinicians practice and check the output against their own judgment. 39% of physicians in the AMA survey asked for exactly that kind of sandbox.

For hospitals and clinics, a view of who has started, who has finished, and how they scored.

In English and Armenian.

What we're not claiming: that it's accredited, that it improves patient outcomes, or that it makes anyone faster. We don't have evidence for those, so we don't say them.

And one limit from the research we've shared. In the 50-country study, working somewhere that actually provides AI tools was more strongly linked to physicians using AI than formal training was. Education is necessary. It isn't the whole answer, and we won't pretend it is.

If you lead clinical education or AI adoption, we'd rather show you a course than describe one. Send us a message.
```

## What changed from posts.md, and why

1. **81% → 72% (posts 1 and 3).** The 81% is "awareness *or* use". Written as
   "81% use AI" it overstates the number, and the evidence file itself says to
   use 72% for a strict claim. A sceptical CMIO would check this first.
2. **Post 4 no longer leaves out the top answers.** Physicians' first choices
   were training inside the EHR (49%) and on the job (46%), both ahead of
   online CE (40%). The old draft left those out, which made the data look
   like it supported us more than it does.
3. **Post 1: "gets none of those" is gone.** It was unsupported, and C6 shows
   most hospitals do evaluate their predictive AI.
4. **Post 2's takeaway is less absolute.** "If all three exist, the tool is
   worth your time" became three questions to ask.
5. **Post 5 now uses voice.md's proof points** ("created with physicians",
   limitations, verification) instead of "reviewed by physicians". The brand
   name is written Darman.ai. The unconfirmed roadmap list is gone.
6. **All five are shorter.** They're now 1,100–1,700 characters. The originals
   ran up to ~2,900, close to LinkedIn's 3,000 limit, with a lot behind
   "see more".
7. **Links moved to a first comment. No em dashes.** US spelling throughout.
   Humanizer scores: posts 1-4 PASS (74-82). Post 5 is REVIEW (62) on
   specificity, which is expected for a product post with no study numbers.
   Don't add numbers to fix it.
9. **Post 4: fairer to OpenEvidence/UpToDate.** OpenEvidence includes a
   structured reflection, so "doesn't test" became "rewards use and
   reflection, not whether you'd catch a wrong answer".
8. **Armenian versions:** not redone. The HY outlines in `posts.md` still use
