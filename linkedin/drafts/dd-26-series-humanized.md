## Post 1 · AI is already in the room

```text
72% of physicians now use AI at work.

That number comes from the AMA's 2026 survey of US physicians. In 2023, it was 38%.

What are they using it for?

Mostly reading and writing.

Summarizing research and standards of care: 39%
Discharge instructions and care plans: 30%
Documentation: 28%
Chart summaries: 28%
Assistive diagnosis: 12%

Diagnosis isn't the main story yet.

Hospitals show the same pattern. In 2024, 71% of US acute care hospitals had predictive AI running inside their EHR. The fastest growing use wasn't clinical either. It was billing, where AI use went from 36% to 61% of hospitals.

So AI didn't enter medicine only through radiology, robotics, or dramatic clinical tools.

It entered through the reading list.
The note.
The chart summary.
The billing workflow.
The quiet places where busy clinicians already needed help.

That's the part leaders can't ignore.

When a tool arrives through a formal launch, someone writes a policy. Someone plans training. Someone decides who can use it and how.

When a tool arrives quietly, it may already be in use by Monday morning.

The question is no longer whether clinicians are using AI.

The question is whether anyone has taught them how far to trust it.

Sources in the first comment.
```

## Post 2 · What the trials actually measured

```text
AI in clinical work now has randomized trial data behind it.

The results are useful.
They're also more modest than the marketing usually sounds.

In breast screening, the MASAI trial randomized 105,934 women in Sweden. AI-supported screening found 6.4 cancers per 1,000 women, compared with 5.0 in standard double reading. False positives didn't rise significantly. Radiologists' screen-reading workload fell by 44%.

That's a real gain.

But the trial's primary endpoint was interval cancers. On that measure, AI was non-inferior. In plain English: not worse, but not proven better.

Documentation has a similar story.

A randomized trial of 238 physicians across 14 specialties tested two ambient AI scribes. One reduced note time by 9.5%. The other didn't make a significant difference. Physicians using either tool reported a modest improvement in work exhaustion.

The authors also found occasional clinically significant inaccuracies. Their conclusion: physicians still need to watch the output.

In Singapore, a study of 169 consultations found that a scribe cut documentation time per visit by 15%. Eye contact with the patient rose from 69.6% to 77.1% of the visit. Total time per patient didn't change.

This is where the evidence seems to be right now:

Less workload in some settings.
More attention on the patient in some settings.
Better detection in specific, well studied workflows.

Not a completely different kind of medicine.

That's still worth having. But only if the person using the tool knows what was tested, what wasn't tested, and where the caveats are.

Before trusting an AI result, ask:

What was the effect size?
Who was it tested on?
What was the catch?
```

## Post 3 · They have it. They were not taught it.

```text
72% of physicians use AI at work.

27% have had no AI training from any source.

Both numbers are from the AMA's 2026 physician survey.

Even among physicians who have had some training, only 11% describe it as "a lot."

The gap isn't just about age. Training rates are close between younger and older physicians: 74% under 35, 71% over 55.

The bigger difference is where they work.

79% of hospital-employed physicians have had some training.
In solo practice, it's 55%.

Globally, the gap looks even wider. In a 2024 survey of 1,049 physicians across 50 countries, 86.5% said they understood AI at least at a basic level. Only 17.7% had formal training.

Physicians with formal training were 3.4 times more likely to use AI in practice. That's an association, not proof that training caused the use. But it's hard to ignore.

The uncomfortable part is that access alone doesn't make people better at using the tool.

In one randomized trial, 50 physicians worked through diagnostic cases with or without an LLM.

With the LLM, they scored 76%.
Without it, they scored 74%.

That difference wasn't significant.

The LLM alone scored 16 points higher than the physicians. So the tool was capable. The doctors were capable. But the combination barely helped.

To be fair, these were written cases, not real patients, and the model is now two generations old. Still, the finding matters.

There's also the risk of skills moving in the wrong direction.

At four endoscopy centers in Poland, adenoma detection during standard colonoscopy, with no AI switched on, fell from 28.4% to 22.4% after AI was introduced.

That study was observational. The authors wrote that AI exposure "might" reduce skill. It doesn't prove it.

But 88% of physicians in the AMA survey already worry about skill loss.

That's the training gap in one sentence:

Having the tool isn't the same as knowing how to use it.

Clinicians need to know what AI is good at, where it fails, and when to overrule it.

92% of physicians say they want more training in exactly that.
```

## Post 4 · What the market offers a clinician in 2026

> **Check before posting:** the Harvard, Coursera, OpenEvidence, UpToDate, Relias and HealthStream details were read on 17 Sep 2026 and not re-verified. Re-open each page before this goes out.

```text
If you're a clinician trying to learn AI in 2026, there are options.

Some are genuinely useful. They just solve different problems.

The AMA has an "AI in Health Care" CME series: six modules, up to 3.5 AMA PRA Category 1 credits. Good for credibility. Good for a shared baseline.

Harvard Medical School offers AI in Clinical Medicine: three live days, $2,500, up to 27 credits. Strong credential, serious depth. But it asks for time and budget.

Stanford's AI in Healthcare specialization on Coursera has five courses, about 48 hours, and can be audited for free. Accessible, but broad. It's not specialty-specific, and it doesn't give CME credit.

Compliance courses, like NAVEX's EU AI Act training, are useful if your organization needs people to understand regulatory obligations. But they aren't really built around clinical judgment.

OpenEvidence and UpToDate Expert AI now award CME credit for using their AI tools. That's low friction and useful. But the credit is tied to use and reflection, not to proving you would catch a wrong answer.

Then there are hospital learning systems like Relias and HealthStream. Many hospitals already use them to assign training and track completion. That part works well. When we checked their public pages in September 2026, we didn't find a clinician-facing AI curriculum.

So the issue isn't that "AI training" doesn't exist.

It does.

The issue is fit.

In the AMA's 2026 survey, physicians said they wanted training inside the EHR first, at 49%. Then on-the-job training, at 46%. Online CE courses came next, at 40%. A hands-on sandbox followed at 39%.

Training from AI companies came last, at 14%.

Almost every choice near the top sits close to real work: inside the EHR, on the job, hands-on.

That's the gap. Not more AI content. Training built around the work clinicians actually do. And, we'd add, around their specialty.
```

## Post 5 · What we built, and why

> **Confirm before posting:** product facts still need confirmation: 70% pass mark, supervisor view, Darman Labs with eight tools, English + Armenian.

```text
The clearest lesson from this series is simple:

The tool isn't the skill.

That's the idea behind Darman.ai.

We built it for clinicians who don't need another abstract course about AI. They need to see how AI behaves inside the kind of work they already do.

So the courses are specialty-specific.

They're created with physicians who show how they actually use generative AI in their field: what they put into the prompt, what they get back, what they check, and when they decide not to use AI at all.

The limitations aren't a footnote.

Verification, failure modes, and the moments when clinical judgment has to take over are part of the training from the start.

The lessons are short enough to finish between patients. There's a final assessment, with 70% needed to pass. Not because a score makes someone an expert, but because passive watching isn't enough.

Darman Labs gives clinicians eight AI tools to practice with. They can test outputs, compare them with their own judgment, and get used to questioning the result instead of just accepting it.

That matters because 39% of physicians in the AMA survey said they want exactly this kind of sandbox.

For hospitals and clinics, there is a supervisor view: who started, who finished, and how they scored.

The product is available in English and Armenian.

Just as important is what we aren't claiming.

We aren't saying it's accredited.
We aren't saying it improves patient outcomes.
We aren't saying it makes clinicians faster.

We don't have evidence for those claims, so we don't make them.

One more thing from the research stayed with us. In the 50-country physician study, working somewhere that actually provides AI tools was more strongly linked to AI use than formal training was.

That feels right.

Education matters. But it's not the whole answer. Clinicians also need access, practice, policy, and space to build judgment.

If you lead clinical education or AI adoption, we'd rather show you the course than describe it.

Send us a message.
```
